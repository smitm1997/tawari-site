The ecological spine

Who connects with whom.

Most field guides stop at a name. Tawari places each organism within its living ecosystem through three essential ecological relationships, wherever a reviewed source backs them.

Feeds on

What it eats, consumes, or pollinates to sustain itself in its habitat.

Eaten by

Which species depend on it for food, and how its energy returns to the soil.

Lives in/on

The biome, host, or substrate where it lives in Colombia.

The app

And when you don't know the name.

  1. Identify in the field.Use practical field keys based on group, size, colour, and habitat to find the right species. Any step can be skipped.
  2. See who matches.If you tell it where you are, only among the species recorded at that place or very near it.
  3. Keep your record in My List.Save your photo, notes, and date in My List. By default the Tawari team can see your sightings too, and uses them to improve the guide; nobody else does.

Can't find what you saw? Log it anyway, with a photo or a description, and the team will try to identify it.

Your personal record

Your personal list: My List.

Every sighting tells a story in the field. Tawari organises your finds in your personal list, ready to check whenever you are on the trail.

Your photo and field notes. Open any sighting in My List to add your photo or edit notes on its behaviour and the place you saw it.

No filler

The card says so.

For many species almost nothing is published. There the card tells only what is known, and invites you to add to it: with your sightings, or by sharing them on iNaturalist.

Named knowledge

Who says it, and where from.

Every written card cites its sources. We would rather name a people and a place than speak of "indigenous people" in general. And no card gives recipes or medical advice.

Where the data comes from

Sources, not opinions.

Colombian sources first, always openly licensed and credited.

See the sources
  • Spanish names: Colombian sources first, such as the Asociación Colombiana de Ornitología's list of bird names (2007), SiB Colombia, the Bogotá Botanical Garden, DAGMA in Cali and the Humboldt Institute; where there is no local one, eBird, GBIF, iNaturalist and Wikipedia.
  • Species and taxonomy: GBIF and eBird.
  • What lives at each place: GBIF and eBird records at those coordinates.
  • Ecological relationships: from GloBI and from manual curation. A GloBI relationship appears when several datasets or several records support it, with a record from Colombia or three independent datasets, or when we have reviewed and approved it. The rest are waiting for review.
  • Photos: Wikimedia Commons, GBIF and iNaturalist, openly licensed only, crediting the author and the licence.
  • Writing: AI helps us draft from cited sources, not invent them. Every written card then goes through an automated review against those sources; toxic and psychoactive species also get a safety screen.

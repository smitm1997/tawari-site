You know that bird lives on your street. Tawari tells you who it is, and who else depends on it.

At Sonso Lagoon the bird that turns up most often in the records is the tropical kingbird: the yellow-and-grey one you have seen a thousand times on a wire, shouting. It hunts on the wing. It chases hawks three times its size out of its territory. That is what Tawari is for — it does not stop at the name.

We start in Valle del Cauca

Meet your neighbours.

A Field-find is the list of who lives in one place. It is not a score: it is a way to meet your neighbours, and you do not have to finish it. There are three, and you can look through them right here without installing anything.

Loading…

All three are in Valle del Cauca, two of them in Cali. The evidence behind each one is very different: at the Botanical Garden the most-recorded species, the arrayancito, has 428 observations; at Cerro de las Tres Cruces the most-recorded has three. The two lists take up the same room on screen and are not worth the same. Tap any species and we tell you how many records it has.

Some species appear without a photograph. Usually because the ones that exist carry licences forbidding commercial use, and an app with a paid tier is exactly that. In nine cases the licence was fine and the herbarium server holding the photo did not answer on the day we built this page. Either way we would rather leave the gap than fill it with a picture of something else.

The ecological spine

Who eats whom.

It is the part no identification app has, and the reason Tawari exists. A card can carry three relationships. These are the harpy eagle's, as the app shows them under Who it lives with:

Feeds on

Three-toed sloths and howler monkeys, taken from the canopy.

Manual curation · confidence 0.95
Returns to ecosystem via

Carrion. An apex predator has nothing hunting it; the slot says how it goes back.

Manual curation · confidence 0.95
Lives in/on

Emergent trees — ceibas — in lowland forest nobody has felled.

Manual curation · confidence 0.95

Today 140 species show their spine. Another thousand-odd relationships are extracted and waiting for review: they are not published until they pass it, because an empty slot beats an invented one.

The app

What happens when you don't know the name.

You saw something. You open the wizard and answer what you can: animal or plant, what silhouette it had, what colours. Any step can be skipped. At the end you get the ones that match — but only from among those recorded at the place you are standing, because a species nobody has seen there does not belong in a list claiming to say what lives there.

When you reach the card you find how to recognise it, when it appears, what eats it, and what it means to the people here. And if you log the sighting it stays in your journal with its date and its place. Nobody else sees it.

When we don't know

The card says so.

For a great many Colombian species there is almost nothing published. When that is the case the card says so and stays short, instead of filling the gap. It shows what is documented — name, taxonomy, records, photographs — and nothing more.

And it tells you how to help: log your sightings with a photo and notes, upload them to iNaturalist too — verified records reach GBIF, the open database science works from — or tell us about a published source.

Knowledge with a name attached

Who says so, and where from.

The jaguar's card cites the cosmology of the Kogi of the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, where the animal — Kaku Shenbuta — joins the spiritual and material worlds, and the ethnographer Gerardo Reichel-Dolmatoff's work on the Muisca of the Bogotá savanna.

When there is no source to cite, the section does not exist. Never "indigenous peoples". Never "traditional medicine". Never preparation instructions or medical claims.

Where the data comes from

Sources, not opinions.

  • Names and taxonomy: SACC, IOC, eBird/Clements, Avibase, GBIF and SiB Colombia.
  • What lives at each place: GBIF and eBird records at those coordinates.
  • Ecological relationships: GloBI and manual curation, each with its confidence.
  • Photos: Wikimedia Commons, GBIF and iNaturalist, openly licensed only, with author credit.
  • Writing: AI helps us draft from cited sources, not invent them. Every written card is reviewed; toxic and psychoactive species get one more safety screen.
